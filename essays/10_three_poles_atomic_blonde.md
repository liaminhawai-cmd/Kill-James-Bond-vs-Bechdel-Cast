# Essay 10 — Three poles, one film: Atomic Blonde and the conservative test
## The proof-of-concept the corpus couldn't run on itself

Everything in this book so far compares two shows that agree politically. That is the design's greatest weakness, and it sits under the book's most theoretically ambitious claim. The affect-grammar inversion (H16) says that affective grammars are *detachable* from political content — that The Bechdel Cast recruits purity-disgust, the pole the disposition literature codes authoritarian, for egalitarian ends. But a detachability claim asserted entirely from within-left data has never actually been tested against the other end of the political spectrum. This chapter is the first test, run as a deliberately small proof-of-concept: one film, a mini-pool of conservative commentary, and a prediction registered in FINDINGS *before* a single conservative sentence was read (Q16), on the model of the Sarkeesian pre-registration that produced this book's best status finding by failing.

Atomic Blonde is the chosen film for the same reason it was the designated H9 thesis-breaker: it is where the two egalitarian shows most *agree* and most *like* the film — a queer female action lead both shows celebrated while convicting the movie of objectifying her. If disposition drives reception, the film the left poles converge on is exactly where a reactionary pole should separate. The prediction, in brief: the conservative pole would (a) condemn the film both shows liked, (b) condemn it through purity-disgust rather than approach-aggression, (c) aim that disgust at the film's gender transgression — the queerness, the "woke" female lead — rather than at the objectification the two shows targeted, and (d) possibly object to the same on-screen material for opposite reasons. The falsifiers were registered alongside: praise, or approach-grammar condemnation, or convergence with the feminist critique itself.

Two honesty notes before the result, both structural. First, **none of these sources is a podcast.** The scouting pass established something that is itself a datum: there is no right-wing analogue of these two shows — no conservative, multi-host, dialogic film podcast doing deep single-film episodes on this canon. The organized right's film criticism lives in print columns, content-advisory sites, and YouTube monologue. The pool therefore came in two tranches: first written prose (a National Review column by Armond White; a Hillsdale Collegian opinion piece by Kayla Stetzel; a Christian Spotlight review by Jonathan Rodriguez, with the genre's scoring apparatus attached), then a set of author-supplied YouTube auto-caption transcripts that restore speech and even some dialogue to the pool — taken up in their own section below. The test remains confined, as registered, to the affect-grammar layer: even the dialogic YouTube material is not a professionalized show whose status economy could be compared with the podcasts'. Second, **no cross-medium rates.** Written prose and unscripted speech have different baseline norms for hedging, profanity, and first person; the podcast counts and these texts never share a table. In fact the within-pool lexicon run (regenerable via `third_pole/third_pole_counts.py`) produced its own methods finding: the frozen speech lexicons *fail to transfer* — the Christian review scores zero on the disgust lexicon while containing the most explicit purity-contamination language in the entire project, because prose carries affect in moral content-vocabulary ("perversion," "immorality") rather than spoken recoil tokens. And the pool's only profanity "hit" is Stetzel *quoting* a slur from Whedon's leaked script — mention, not use. Function, not lexical shadow, one more time, at maximum strength. The coding below is therefore hand-coding, the unit this project always falls back to when the counters go blind.

### The result: "the right" is not one disposition

The headline is that the registered prediction was half right, and the half it got wrong is the finding. The prediction treated the conservative pole as one thing. The pool immediately split into three sub-poles with three different affect grammars — and the split runs along exactly the line the Schröder/Haidt framework would draw, which sharpens the theory rather than rescuing it.

**The religious-conservative pole runs the predicted purity-disgust grammar, verbatim.** The Christian Spotlight review's moral verdict is "Extremely Offensive," and its summary counsel is quarantine: keep the teenagers away, "let pass by unseen," and — the sentence that could have been written by the purity literature itself — the hope that the review will keep readers from "fouling their minds" with "temptations of the eye, perversion." This is contamination language: the film is a substance that fouls, the mind a vessel to be kept clean, the correct response *distance*. The site's own apparatus reinforces it — the review is tagged to explainers on "lasciviousness" and "the consequences of sexual immorality," and the sex/nudity line item leads with "graphic lesbian sex." A reader comment on the page supplies the folk version: "More like a lesbian porno!" Here, prediction (c) also lands: the disgust attaches to the film's sexual content and its queerness, not to the objectification-as-such that the feminist shows targeted.

**The secular-conservative pole runs a completely different grammar: contempt at ideology.** Armond White's National Review column contains, remarkably, *no purity-disgust at all* — the lesbian seduction passes in a parenthetical quip, without a flicker of revulsion. His entire affective register is erudite derision aimed at the film's *politics as fashion*: "political costume-play," heroines who are "products of these self-deluding times," the whole enterprise "trendy rather than serious." The register is high-certainty, zero-hedge, diminutive-coining ("killer-chick"), and — this is worth pausing on — it runs a *systems engine*. White locates the film in film-historical space (the long-take style measured against Gun Crazy, the corridor fight against Oldboy), reads it as an industrial product ("How much triteness can Hollywood unload"), and theorises its audience ("envisioning them as easily excitable adolescent boys" — a dark cousin of both shows' recruitment-model theorising, H17a). Structurally, White is closer to KJB than to his own religious co-partisans: approach-grammar, object/system analysis, contempt rather than recoil. What differs is the target — where KJB prosecutes the film's sexism, White prosecutes its feminism.

**The traditionalist pole produced the result the prediction flagged as a falsifier — convergence with the feminist critique itself.** The Hillsdale piece condemns the film under an anti-feminist headline ("feminism is toxic for society") and then, in its body, executes an almost point-for-point Bechdel Cast reading. It names the male gaze explicitly ("each was reduced to an object of the male gaze"). It objects to "needless slow pans over Charlize's barely dressed body" — the same shots both podcasts named, one of which (the wire-tape dressing shot, the slow pans) anchored the essay-05 convergence. It reads the film as "a male fantasy brought to fruition" and a "weaponized Barbie," defends LGBTQ representation against the "girl-on-girl" fantasy, cites the San Diego State women-in-film study with load-bearing statistics (29 percent of leads, 7 percent of directors — citation-as-source, precisely BC's H12 practice), condemns Joss Whedon's leaked script as written "from perspective of a leering male," praises Patty Jenkins — and lands on the *female-director counterfactual*, the exact argument both KJB and BC made about this exact film: until the industry admits "female directors within the genre, the cycle of weaponized Barbies will persist." Even the deep charge is shared: the film's feminism is *marketing* — "capitalizing on the feminist 'brand,'" "shameful the film was marketed as a feminist success" — which is, almost clause for clause, the objection KJB's SCUM instrument scored as record-level Smarm and BC voiced as betrayed hope. The only thing that separates the Hillsdale reading from the two podcasts is the frame it hangs the shared analysis on: where they say *this film fails feminism*, she says *this film's fake feminism is what's toxic* — and notably, her own text argues the first thesis while her headline sells the second.

### What this does to the registered prediction

Scored against the pre-registered text, with no retrofitting: **(a) valence flip — confirmed**, every conservative source condemns the film both shows liked, so politics does predict verdict-valence here. **(b) purity-disgust grammar — confirmed but decomposed**, and the decomposition is the real result: the disgust signature belongs to the *religious* sub-pole specifically, while the secular-political right runs approach-contempt. In Haidt's terms — the terms Schröder builds on — disgust tracks elevated *sanctity/purity foundations*, which are characteristic of religious conservatism in particular, not of right-politics as such. The prediction's error was collapsing a foundations-level claim into a left/right binary. Corrected, the theory gets sharper, not weaker: affect grammar tracks *moral-foundation profile*, and political poles on both sides contain multiple profiles. **(c) target flip — partially falsified**, the productive falsification: only the religious pole aims at queerness-as-contamination; the traditionalist pole aims at the very objectification the feminist shows target. **(d) same-object, opposite-grammar — confirmed and richer than predicted.** The sexualisation of Theron's body is condemned from every pole in the study, through four distinguishable grammars: feminist objectification-critique (BC, pedagogical disgust-tinged), feminist objectification-critique (KJB, approach-contempt at the film's smarm), traditionalist harm-to-society critique (Hillsdale, earnest, "toxic to society's view of women"), and purity-contamination critique (Christian Spotlight, "temptations of the eye"). Same shots. Four moral grammars. One conclusion — the camera treats this woman as a thing — reached by all of them, *for four different reasons*. **(e) register** — the reactionary in-group binding predicted for the pool appears only faintly (White's "Hollywood condescends to most moviegoers"), and mostly the prose genre swamps it; not decidable on this pool.

### The instrument finding nobody ordered

The most delightful result in the pool was not predicted at all. The Christian Spotlight review runs a **two-axis scoring instrument** — Moral Rating ("Extremely Offensive") separated from Moviemaking Quality (favourable) — and the reviewer is explicit that the axes dissociate: judged "on the action filmmaking merits alone," he'd welcome a sequel; judged on content, the film should pass unseen. Attached is an itemised audit: "f-words (40+)," the profanity enumerated token by token, categories for violence, sex/nudity, smoking, alcohol, each graded. Set this beside SCUM's four axes and comparability database, the Bechdel test and the nipple scale, and Common Sense Media's age-banded category flags (included in the archive as the advisory-genre control, not coded conservative), and the H8 finding generalises across the political spectrum in one step: **every one of these interpretive communities builds a multi-axis scoring instrument, and every instrument encodes what its community needs to measure.** SCUM weights smarm because KJB's community prosecutes self-satisfaction; the nipple scale weights representation-progress because BC's community teaches it; Christian Spotlight's moral axis weights contamination-exposure because its community practises avoidance. The verdicts differ because the instruments weigh different things — which is precisely how essay 05 dissolved the one apparent verdict-split between KJB and BC. The instrument-relativity of verdicts is not a two-show quirk. It looks like a general property of communities that score films, and the Christian site even mirrors this project's own method, counting profanity tokens as a moral audit exactly as the tagging pipeline counts them as register data. The same count, in two instruments, measuring two different things: there may be no cleaner demonstration in the book that a number means nothing without the instrument around it.

### The approach/avoidance axis at its most literal

One more generalisation falls out, and it is the chapter's best gift to the synthesis. The Christian review's bottom line is *avoidance in the literal, physical sense*: do not see the film; keep your children from it; let it "pass by unseen." The disapproved object is handled by quarantine. Now set that against what Kill James Bond *is*: a show whose entire format consists of deliberately, repeatedly consuming the media it disapproves of, at length and on purpose, in order to prosecute it — approach toward the toxic object as a working method. The Bechdel Cast sits between: watch it, teach it, flag the contamination. The approach/avoidance axis that Chapter II generalised from affect to status here completes itself at the level of *media-consumption strategy*: the purity pole avoids the object, the approach pole seeks it out to fight it, and the pedagogical pole supervises contact with it. Three dispositions, three relationships to the disapproved artefact — and each community's format (avoidance-advisory, prosecution-podcast, teaching-podcast) is its disposition made institutional.

### The speech pool: the grassroots check

After the print coding was done, the author supplied a second tranche: auto-caption transcripts of four YouTube takes on the same film — speech-native, male-hosted, two of them dialogic, which is exactly the material the print pool couldn't provide (archived with provenance and coding notes in `third_pole/atomic_blonde/`; caption bleeping masks profanity, so those counts are floors, never rates). The four span the disposition space on their own: a three-host casual crew with a matinee/rental verdict system; a two-host young pair coded reactionary-adjacent from its own discourse ("the movie's gay" as a pejorative; the lesbian scene dismissed as pandering; the "I'm not trying to be sexist here, but… biologically" disclaimer); a solo action-genre purist with Hard Target and Cobra posters on the wall; and a queer-positive left video-essayist ("Ren," with a guest segment), included deliberately as the left YouTube pole. What the grassroots material does to the findings is confirm every one of them and sharpen two.

First, the purity-disgust decomposition survives its hardest grassroots test. If disgust were the general grammar of the right rather than the signature of the sanctity foundation, the reactionary-adjacent pair is where it should surface — young men condemning the film's queerness in casual unguarded speech. It doesn't. Their condemnation runs entirely through *contempt and irritation* — "garbage," "obnoxious," "pissed me off," pandering-accusation ("the only reason they had a lesbian sex scene was just to get people talking") — with a disgust-lexicon rate of effectively zero. Even where the pejorative is literally "gay," the affect attached to it is dismissal, not recoil. The contamination grammar remains, across seven sources now, exclusive to the religious pole.

Second, the registered prediction's appetitive branch — that the same sexualisation the feminist shows critique might be met on the male-audience side with plain enjoyment — is confirmed almost word for word. The action purist: the lesbian scene was the only twist he liked — "She's into women, I'm into women… We see some titties, but hey." The dialogic crew runs the more interesting middle case: appetite *with a flicker of the critique inside it* — "she makes it hot," but also, unprompted, "it's such a fetishized sexual aspect" and "the violence is almost like a fetish," the crew half-noticing the exact structure BC and KJB prosecute, from inside the enjoying position. So the one scene now sorts six ways: feminist critique-plus-appetite (KJB, BC), toxic-male-fantasy critique (Hillsdale), purity-contamination (Christian Spotlight), ideological contempt-in-passing (White), plain appetite (the purist), and appetite-noticing-itself (the crew).

Third — and this is the H9 payload — the *queer* YouTube essayist converges with the two podcasts to a degree that is almost eerie for an independent source in a different genre: she names the male gaze on the sex scene while defending the romance's sincerity (critique-plus-appetite, the exact both-hands position both shows took), names *bury your gays* on Delphine's death, objects to the eroticised framing of the strangulation ("it just feels weirdly eroticized" — the fridging-in-lingerie reading, independently derived), and calls the dead-lover beat "a trope I simply could have done without." She also runs unmistakably systems-engine moves — the Tarkovsky-poster analysis, the East–West "paradox" reading, the DEFA gay-cinema Easter egg — which is a caution against mapping the engines onto disposition too neatly: framework-building shows up wherever a critic has deep domain investment, on the left as well as in White's film-historical contempt. And across *all seven* sources, left to right, professional to amateur, one layer converges completely: the craft reading. Everyone names the staircase fight as the centrepiece; everyone praises the fatigue-and-damage realism (the crew: "getting her ass kicked just like anybody else"; Ren: directors usually "prioritize pretty over gritty"); nearly everyone reaches the John-Wick-derivative frame and the too-convoluted-plot verdict. The dissociation could not be cleaner: **what a film's craft is doing converges across every disposition in the pool; what the film's women and queerness *mean* is precisely where the poles split, and each pole splits along its own moral-foundation grammar.** That is H9 and H16 in one sentence, finally with the political variance the two-show corpus could never supply.

### Disciplines, and what this pool cannot say

The hold-downs, stated plainly. This is seven texts on one film (three conservative print, four YouTube speech) — a proof-of-concept, not a corpus; nothing here is "the right's reading of cinema," only an existence proof of differentiated grammars. Genre confounds disposition throughout (opinion columns reward contempt; advisory reviews reward inventory; solo YouTube rewards first-person sprawl — the speech files run 23–51 first-person-singular per thousand, which is genre, not psychology). Disposition was coded from each text's own discourse, with venue as prior only — and the Hillsdale case shows why that discipline matters, since the text's actual grammar (earnest, hedged, first-person, study-citing) sits far from its venue's politics and its own headline; the same goes for "reactionary-adjacent," which describes the pair's discourse, not a party card. The pool is contemporaneous with the film's 2017 release, unlike the podcast episodes, so era is uncontrolled here. The dialogic gap is now only *partly* filled: the crew and the pair are dialogic male speech, but neither is a professionalized political film podcast, so the original scouting datum stands in narrowed form — the *organized* right does film criticism in monologue and print, and the dialogic male film-talk that does exist at the grassroots is casual-normie rather than politically articulated. And one asymmetry must be named: the scouting search-space was itself constrained (this session's network policy blocked most conservative-media domains at full-text level; every full text was supplied by the author), so the pool is a convenience sample, not a survey. None of these caveats touches the existence claims — that a purity-disgust grammar, an ideology-contempt grammar, a converging objectification-critique, and a contempt-without-disgust grassroots grammar all live to the right of the two shows — because existence claims are what a small pool can prove.

### What the proof-of-concept proves

It proves the test is worth running, and it returns the H16 detachability thesis strengthened in a way the two-show corpus could never have managed. Within the left, the corpus showed the "authoritarian-coded" disgust grammar serving egalitarian politics (BC). The third pole now shows the mirror: on the right, the "egalitarian-coded" approach-contempt grammar serves reactionary politics (White, and the grassroots pair in its unpolished register), while the disgust grammar lives specifically where the sanctity foundation is elevated (the religious pole — and *only* there, across seven sources), and a traditionalist writer can run the *left's own objectification analysis* inside an anti-feminist frame (Hillsdale). Affect grammars cross political lines in both directions. They are tools, not tribal markers — which is the strongest form of the detachability claim, and it was reached the right way: through a registered prediction whose most interesting clause failed. The speech tranche then added the two dissociations that complete the picture: the same on-screen scene sorting six ways along the disposition space, and the craft layer converging across every pole while the meaning layer splits — one film, one shared perceptual reading, and a fan of moral grammars opening out from it. Where this arm goes, if the author wants the full version, is more films (the masculine canon, where coverage is densest at every pole). But as a proof-of-concept the chapter has done its job: the book's central theoretical claim has now been outside its own political bubble and come back confirmed, complicated, and better specified.
