# Essay 05 — Atomic Blonde (2017)
## The thesis-breaker that didn't break

For four essays the convergence thesis has been hardening without ever being properly tested. The shows reach the same readings, yes — but on a Bond film, a Bond parody, a heist. None of those forced the issue, because none of them put the two shows' deepest divergences directly on the same object. Atomic Blonde does. It is a queer female action lead, with a lesbian sex scene shot by the cinematographer of *Transformers: Dark of the Moon*, and it lands precisely where Kill James Bond's camp authority and open appetite should collide head-on with The Bechdel Cast's male-gaze audit. If the thesis — *one analysis, two rhetorical economies* — is going to break anywhere, it breaks here. I went in expecting it to. It didn't.

What Atomic Blonde produced instead is more useful than a clean break would have been: the convergence held on the feminist *substance* down to the individual camera shot, while the film exposed the single cleanest *seam* between the shows in the whole corpus so far — a seam that runs not through their conclusions but through which engine processes the film and how their instruments are built. That is a better result than divergence, because it tells us exactly what kind of difference the difference between these shows actually is.

### The pair

KJB's is S2E22, April 2023 — the show is banking episodes before travelling to London for the Austin Powers live run (essay 03), which dates it tightly. It is, by the hosts' own running scoreboard, one of the two worst films they have ever covered: it scores 28 on the SCUM system, tied with *Live and Let Die*. BC's is the Vanessa Guerrero episode, datable to roughly 2020 by its references to *Birds of Prey* and *The Old Guard* as recent touchstones in an action renaissance. Guerrero — host of the action-and-horror podcast *Kicking and Screaming*, and, as she puts it, "extremely queer" — is the most domain-expert guest in the corpus to date: where Sarkeesian (still ahead, essay 06) will outrank the hosts on feminist criticism, Guerrero outranks them on the action genre itself. That matters for what BC is able to do here, as we'll see.

A note on the numbers, all regenerable from `tagging/counts.json`. For the first time in the corpus, **KJB hedges more than BC** — 10.4 against 9.8 epistemic hedges per 1k words. The hedging asymmetry doesn't just shrink here (as it did on Ocean's), it inverts. The cause is plain in both transcripts: the plot is genuinely incomprehensible, and both shows say so at length. BC "had to google atomic blonde ending explained"; KJB runs an entire recurring bit of a host playing their own baffled mother ("I'm my mum at this point — I don't understand"). When a film supplies real uncertainty, KJB engages it with real hedging, and the theory-of-the-listener mechanism that usually makes BC the hedgier show (teaching the imagined newcomer) is swamped. H2's magnitude was already known to be pair-variable; Atomic Blonde shows it can cross zero. The directional claim survives only as a tendency, not a law.

### The convergence, shot for shot

Set the two episodes beside each other and the overlap on the feminist reading is, once again, near-total — and this time it is granular to a degree that rules out coincidence.

Both shows independently identify the film's **two-mode treatment of Charlize Theron's body**: realistic and powerful inside the fight choreography, sexualised and gazey everywhere else. And both reach for the *same single shot* to make the point — the moment Lorraine tapes a wire to herself and the camera travels from her pelvis up her body, lingering at the bra. BC's Caitlin describes it almost frame by frame and notes it was lifted into the trailer; KJB's hosts flag the same "feeding the wire through under her bra" shot as "blatantly exploitative." Two sets of critics, on different continents, three years apart, freeze on the identical few seconds of a two-hour film.

Both land the **"equal opportunity lusting" demand** in nearly the same words. KJB: if you are going to shoot a body that way, do it to the men too — "they should have done that to" the others; one host protests that a heavyset Russian woman who could kill them got no such treatment and "I would have come in my fucking pants." BC: "James McAvoy's right there" in a mesh tank top, "Bill Skarsgård, give me some of that," "equal opportunity lusting." Both shows, that is, hold the male-gaze *critique* and erotic *appetite* simultaneously — the very combination I expected to split them. It doesn't split them; they both do it.

Both diagnose **Delphine's death as fridging**, and both fix on the same damning detail: she is killed in lingerie, strangled so the body stays "pretty." BC names the trope outright (bury-your-gays), quotes the director calling the gender-flip "provocative," and argues the queer relationship was made "not in an informed way." KJB independently calls the scene "sadistic, cynical," notes the film "justifying itself" by letting her stab back a little, and proposes the same fix BC implies — she should have died "the way any of the men in the movie" died, without the lingering. Both even reach the **body-shape double standard** as a general law: KJB spells it out ("look at the body-shape diversity in the male cast" against the uniformly-hot women), and BC arrives at the same place through the casting and costume critique.

And both close on the **same summary judgment of craft**: a film that is "less than the sum of its parts" (KJB) / a "stepping stone" whose pieces outclass its whole (BC), which a woman in a senior creative role — a female writer, specifically — would have changed. The H9 standing challenge was to find one pair where the shows' conclusions genuinely diverge. On the film most able to deliver it, the substantive conclusions converge harder than on any previous pair. The thesis doesn't just survive; it tightens.

### The seam: same film, different engine, different instrument

So where is the divergence? It is real, and it is the clearest the corpus has shown — but it is not a disagreement about the film. It runs along two fault lines, and naming them precisely is the point of this essay.

**Different engine.** KJB spends its entire final third on material BC never touches: the film as a piece of *intelligence-services self-mythology*. The "American spy all along" twist becomes a meditation on Cold War whitewashing — the way the genre retroactively justifies its own violence by making everything resolve into one unbroken, purposeful line ("the spying has always been justified… it's a fine piece of intelligence-services mythologising, the Bond movies do it too"). From there KJB runs its native systems engine at full throttle: Britain as "a shit country for pricks" whose decline makes it a site for others to exploit, America's "real country forever" self-myth, and a hard pivot to present-day police violence ("how many times do we have to see police engaging in some random act of violence before we go, maybe that's not true"). This is the object/system reading (H7) doing exactly what it does — routing the film through state, history, and institution. BC's political reading, by contrast, stops at honest bafflement: "there's so much I don't know about the Cold War," with a link to "the Cold War explained for children." BC does not process the film as a political object because that is not BC's engine; BC processes it as *representation* — the bi-visibility, the bury-your-gays trope, the question of whether a female writer would have served the material. Same film, two different machines, neither of which converts to the other's output. This is the divergence the convergence thesis predicted all along: not in conclusions, but in which questions each economy is built to ask.

**Different instrument, different verdict valence.** Here is the one place the shows genuinely diverge in their bottom line — and it is instructive that it is a divergence in *score*, not in reading. KJB rates Atomic Blonde among the two worst films it has ever covered (28/28 on SCUM); BC gives it a relatively warm three nipples and an affectionate send-off. They agree almost completely about what the film does to women, and they still land on opposite sides of the ledger — because their instruments measure different things. SCUM weights Smarm heavily, and Atomic Blonde's chief offence to KJB is its *self-pleased style*: the Spotify-best-of-the-80s soundtrack, the graphic-novel preening, the "so fucking pleased with itself" surface earns a 7 for smarm before misogyny is even scored. The nipple scale measures representation-as-step-forward, and on that axis a female-led, queer-inclusive, female-produced action hit registers as progress worth three nipples even with its failures logged. The divergence in valence is therefore not a disagreement about the movie; it is an artifact of instrument design. This is the strongest possible confirmation of the corrected H8 — *both shows hold instruments; what differs is what the instrument is built to weigh* — and it means even the one real verdict-split in the corpus reduces, on inspection, to the convergence thesis rather than against it.

**Different affect — and a dormant grammar.** KJB enforces through contempt: SCUM 28, prosecutorial fury at a "hack" film, the approach-aggression the corpus has tracked throughout (anger-lexicon 8 to BC's 0). BC's affect here is the surprise. It is not disgust. BC *likes* Atomic Blonde — and so the purity-disgust enforcement grammar that essays 01–04 tracked simply goes quiet (disgust-lexicon 5, mostly aimed at vodka-on-ice). This is a genuinely useful refinement to H16: BC's purity-disgust is an *enforcement* grammar deployed against disapproved objects, not a constant affective baseline. When BC approves, the grammar is dormant, replaced by an appreciative-ambivalent register. Die Hard (essay 07) was already queued to test the unexamined cell of "disgust-grammar with nothing to quarantine"; Atomic Blonde has partly answered it in advance — the grammar isn't suppressed effortfully, it's simply not invoked, because there is nothing to quarantine.

### The H11 pair: constitutive inside-ness vs imported inside-ness

Atomic Blonde is also the corpus's definitive specimen for queer-reading epistemics (H11), and it refines the hypothesis in a way no earlier pair could.

KJB asserts from inside, uniformly and constitutively. All three hosts read the lesbian scene with confident camp authority — the "fem-for-femme with a clear top/bottom dynamic, legible to straight men" analysis, the bisexual-lighting riff, the flat first-person ownership of the appetite ("it's not exploitation if I think it's hot"). They also route the film's *characters* through their own gender — James McAvoy's feral hobo-spy "is a bit gender for me," Theron's tailoring "was gender to me," "we're learning some things about ourselves." The queer reading is not a stance the show adopts toward the material; it is the show's default voice, the same assert-from-inside position the brief logged pre-corpus.

BC is more interesting, because BC splits. The *house* register — Caitlin's — processes the queer content through pedagogy and catalogue, exactly as it processes everything: "we get some bi visibility," the bury-your-gays trope defined and explained, bisexual erasure taught to the imagined newcomer. This is the defer-and-teach-from-outside pole. But the *lived* inside view is supplied by the guest: Guerrero brings the first-person queer desire the house register doesn't ("I love watching girls make out"; "when do I get to lust over an action star that isn't a guy?"), and a genuinely inside reading of queer death-scenes (her Bride of Chucky analysis — a queer death that doesn't read as punishment *because the writer was gay* — which is, structurally, the exact argument KJB makes from inside about everything).

So H11 sharpens: **KJB's inside-ness is constitutive; BC's is imported.** KJB speaks from inside queerness as its baseline; BC's baseline addresses the newcomer *about* queerness pedagogically and accesses the lived inside view by casting a queer guest. This maps cleanly onto the theory-of-the-listener (H2) and the identification axis (H6): BC's whole architecture assumes an audience that needs the trope explained, and reaches the inside perspective the way it reaches every perspective — through the rotating guest chair — while KJB assumes an initiate audience already living inside the position. The "defer-outside vs assert-inside" distinction holds, but at the level of *house register*; the guest mechanism is how BC's format does inside-ness without changing its register. It is the same machine the brief described, running on queerness.

### What this pair settles

It settles the convergence thesis, in the strongest available test. The feminist substance converges even on the queer-female-action film; the divergence is in engine (KJB's systems/political read has no BC counterpart) and in instrument design (SCUM weighs smarm, the nipple scale weighs representational progress, producing opposite scores from the same reading) — which means the book's central claim should now be stated at full strength: *the two shows perform one feminist analysis through two rhetorical economies, and where their bottom-line verdicts diverge, the divergence traces to what their instruments are built to measure, not to disagreement about the film.* That is the strange, strong claim the convergence chapter has been circling, and Atomic Blonde licenses it.

It also hands the synthesis its cleanest H11 specimen (constitutive vs imported inside-ness) and a real refinement to H16 (BC's disgust is a deployable enforcement grammar, dormant on approval, not a baseline). The remaining standing challenge — a pair where the *readings*, not the scores, diverge — is now unmet at ×5, and the films most able to produce it are behind us. The honest expectation going into the back half of the corpus is that it stays unmet, and that the book's job is to explain *why* two such different machines keep producing the same analysis. The next essay tests the one variable that has never moved: a guest who outranks the hosts in their own domain. The prediction is already registered.
