And so, this is a good place to acknowledge that the developmental conditions which diminished
the aggressiveness of the left had a corresponding set of effects on the right, and on everyone
else in society.
To describe these effects requires either breathtakingly simple, intuitive description,
or a complex synthesis of a vast scientific landscape.
To use the former, people got less tough.
This can be seen in the huge number of accounts by people who consider themselves civilized
of people they consider primitive, in which the extraordinary toughness of the uncivilized
is described, often with a mixture of wonder and horror.
But of course, I won't leave it there.
To invoke a little bit of this vast scientific landscape, and to make the statement more
rigorous, the broad set of changes that occurred in the entire population exposed to our increasingly
technological, consumer-oriented environment, one manifestation of which was diminished
aggression on the left, can be characterized generally as behavioral dysregulation resulting
from alterations of the brain's reward circuitry, a consequence of living in a society with
too many easily accessed, compulsive pleasures.
It's pretty interesting to see the science that points toward the behaviorally debilitating
effects of civilization itself refuse to acknowledge its own implications.
Somehow, smartphones and social media were an exception.
A flurry of papers and popular communications described how these particular aspects of
our civilization were altering the dopamine circuitry in people's brains, producing changes
as seen through various brain imaging techniques identical to those seen in drug addiction, diminishing
the capacity to put in the effort to achieve rewards.
But it was very rare for anyone to extend this logic to all the other facets of a
civilization centrally predicated on cheap pulses of dopamine, on high rewards for low
effort with their consequent changes to the neural circuitry that governs our motivation,
pleasure, and tolerance for difficulty in the pursuit of rewarding stimuli.
And this was true even in the case of papers that examined a panoply of conditions that
all both had a formal description as a pathology in a text like the Diagnostic Statistical Manual
and whose incidence was skyrocketing.
A 2017 paper in Frontiers and Biosciences characteristic, it lists dozens of disorders
from internet gambling addiction to body dysmorphia to ADHD to narcissistic personality disorder
to autism, which are all characterized as involving reward deficiency syndrome, a syndrome of alterations
to the brain's dopamine circuitry, and which are all rapidly becoming more common without
ever making the logical inference that our civilization itself is causing alterations
to the brain's reward circuits.
A notable exception was Peter Sterling, who understood that a basic question we had to ask ourselves
in Assessing Prospects for Survival was how consumer civilization affects the neurobiology underlying our behavior.
