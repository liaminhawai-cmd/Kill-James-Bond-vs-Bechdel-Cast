Hans Berger's research into telepathy suffered from this fundamental flaw.
It was all tele and no pathia.
However, in searching for a medium through which thought might be transmitted across
great distances, Berger became the first person to make an electroencephalogram, or
EEG.
How one could stumble upon such a device in this manner is fairly self-evident.
EEGs, after all, measure brain activity outside of the skull.
The signaling activity of an individual neuron consists of a change in the
voltage differential between its interior and exterior.
In the cumulative electrical activity of the billions of neurons in a brain,
registers beyond the brain's boundaries, perturbing the surrounding
electromagnetic field.
This perturbation characteristically registers as a series of waves,
indicating significant synchronization of neural firing.
It was Berger who coined the familiar term, brain waves.
For as much as brain imaging has advanced greatly since the development of the EEG,
it still gives us truly fundamental insights.
Differences in the wave structure can distinguish between consciousness and
unconsciousness, for instance.
Thus, Berger's path began with an extreme state of consciousness, the experience
of imminent and potentially mortal danger, which led to the temporary induction
of an unusual ability, his apparent telepathic communication with his sister,
and progressed into an important scientific discovery.
He is far from alone in this.
Scientists have always achieved many of their greatest insights as a result of
visionary experiences.
For centuries, however, this reality was sufficiently far beyond the parameters
of acceptable discourse that it was simply ignored.
The anecdotes about such visions that accumulated during this time were
countless.
For instance, Srinivasa Ramanujan, widely cited as the most talented
mathematician to ever live, claimed that he did not reason his way to his many
stunning mathematical insights, but rather that a family deity would write
formulas on his tongue and bestow mathematical intuitions in his dreams.
August Kukule discovered the structure of benzene, a ring-shaped molecule
consisting of six carbons joined by alternating single and double bonds
when he had a vision of a serpent devouring its own tail, the Ouroboros.
His 1865 paper describing benzene structure was of considerable
significance for both pure and applied chemistry.
In his revelatory experience was sometimes called Kukule's dream,
although he was not asleep when it occurred.
